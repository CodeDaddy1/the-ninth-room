Turn 14 · Six ways to draw the arch

The archway, six ways

All six keep what makes 13c work: an arch seen straight on, warm light inside, the wall's thickness thrown as shadow. They differ on how much light there is, whether depth is drawn or implied, and whether the nine-rooms idea comes back. Each shown as a lockup and then at three real sizes.

Full light

Light fills most of the opening. Brightest and most inviting of the six, and the yellow is large enough to be the channel's colour signature at a glance. Its cost: at small sizes it is mostly a yellow blob, so the arch outline stops doing much work.

14b

Deep set

The Ninth Room
Nine rooms. One you can't find.
60 · 32 · 20px tall

Concentric arches draw the wall's depth instead of implying it, so the light sits further back — you are looking down a passage, not at a doorway. The most architectural of the six and the closest in feel to the reference. Three nested strokes is a lot to hold at 20px, where it collapses to a simple lit arch.

14c

Sliver

The Ninth Room
Nine rooms. One you can't find.
60 · 32 · 20px tall

The door only just ajar — a vertical sliver of light and the rest in shadow. Quietest and most mysterious, and it matches the channel's premise more honestly than a wide-open arch does: you are being shown a crack, not given a tour. It is also the most restrained use of yellow in the whole system, which may make it too quiet as a channel avatar.

14d

Nine arches

The Ninth Room
Nine rooms. One you can't find.
68 · 37 · 23px tall

Brings the nine-rooms idea back without losing the arch: a colonnade of nine openings, one lit. This is the only variation that explains the name on sight, and it makes a genuinely good repeated pattern for banners and end cards. Two trades: no single arch is big enough to carry a figure, so the human scale goes, and the unlit arches need lifting to #3E6D9C to stay visible at all — which makes this the only variation where the eight dark rooms are a real presence rather than a background. At the smallest size the eight unlit arches are effectively texture, so below 23px use a single lit arch instead.

14e

Long shadow

The Ninth Room
Nine rooms. One you can't find.
60 · 32 · 20px tall

The figure steps out and the light throws a shadow across the floor beyond the arch. Adds a second beat — arrival rather than entry — and it is the only version with any motion in it. The shadow breaks the square bounding box, so it needs a padded lockup and cannot be used as a tight avatar crop; below 48px it drops and this becomes 14a.

14f

Numbered

A nine knocked out of the light, sitting in the arch like a room number on a gallery door. The shadow is pulled back to the right shoulder so the whole numeral stays in the lit zone — the nine and the wall depth cannot share the same space, and the nine wins. Says the name without spelling it, and gives the mark a hard edge the pure arch lacks; the cost is the figure, and with it the human scale. The numeral drops below 32px, where the mark reverts to a plain lit arch.

My read

14b is closest to the reference and the most beautiful large. 14d is the only one that explains the name on sight and the best system-wide, since nine arches tile into banners and end cards. 14c is the most on-premise — a crack of light, not a tour. 14f is the most practical if the mark has to work without the wordmark.

The real trade

Every version that keeps the figure loses something else — a nine, nine arches, or depth. The figure is what makes the mark feel like a person walking into somewhere, so it is worth protecting unless the mark has to survive at 20px on its own, in which case drop it and take 14f.

Turn 13 · Six marks, one palette

Six ways to draw the ninth room

All six use the Cyanotype palette and the same wordmark, so the only variable is the mark itself. Each is shown as a lockup, then at 56 / 32 / 20px — because the real test is whether it still reads as a favicon and a watermark, not whether it looks good large. 13a is the one already in the kit, included for comparison.

Solid rooms, one lit. Holds at 20px because it is nine blocks and nothing else — the safest of the six. Its weakness is that it reads as a generic grid until you know the name; the mark carries no idea on its own.

13b

Key plan

The Ninth Room
Nine rooms. One you can't find.
56 · 32 · 20px

The same plan, drawn instead of filled — eight empty rooms in cyan outline, the ninth solid. Truest to the no-plates rule the whole kit runs on, and the yellow reads far louder because it is the only filled shape. Watch it at 20px: the hairlines start to merge, so the small-size version needs the stroke bumped.

An arched doorway seen straight on: a cyan hairline outline, warm light filling the opening, and the thickness of the wall thrown as a shadow across the right side so the arch reads as a real passage rather than a shape. A single silhouette at the threshold gives it scale and makes the mark about a person walking in. The figure drops below 48px and the shadow carries it the rest of the way down — the only mark here that gains meaning at large sizes instead of just surviving small ones.

A museum wall label: the room number in brackets, exactly as the kit already brackets speaker tags. The most institutional and the one that would look most at home printed on a card next to an exhibit. At 20px the brackets drop and it becomes a two-character monogram, which is a legitimate small-size mark rather than a degraded one.

My read

13b is the honest upgrade to what you already have — same idea, drawn in the kit's own language, and the yellow finally means something because it is the only fill. 13c is the one with a story in it — and now the only one with a person in it. 13f is the most usable across print, avatars and merch. 13e is the prettiest and the least on-brief.

Worth knowing

Nothing here changes the wordmark or the palette, so switching marks is a contained change — the watermark, the end plate and the avatars, and nothing else in the kit. These are drawn in HTML at the sizes shown; the production marks would need to be redrawn as real SVG before handoff.
